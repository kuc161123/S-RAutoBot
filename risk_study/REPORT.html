Quantitative risk study · commissioned 2026-08-11

Can risk-per-trade be raised without unacceptable drawdown?

A full re-derivation of the sizing question for the live Bybit divergence bot — engine audited, two bugs fixed, execution cost measured from real fills rather than assumed, and every result reported separately on in-sample and genuinely untouched data.

32,913 trades · 2023-06-01 → 2026-07-25 277 symbols / 728 configs 9 agents base commit 263a4ff rev 2 · live exit rule, $1,500
Revision 2 — the first pass modelled the wrong exit
Challenged on whether the sweep matched the bot exactly. It did not. Corrected — and the conclusion got stronger, not weaker.

Revision 1 resolved every trade against a fixed take-profit. The bot has trailed since 2026-08-02 (risk.trailing_stop.enabled: true), and it ran on a $1,500 account, not the $10,000 the brief specified. Both are now modelled. The taper rungs are denominated in dollars, so starting balance is not a scale-free choice: at $10,000 the taper had already cut base risk from 0.30% to 0.22% before the sweep varied anything.

Under the honest CHOP gate the trailing stop costs 0.187 R/trade — it cuts net expectancy from +0.246 R to +0.058 R. A four-fold smaller edge supports a four-fold smaller bet, so the in-sample optimum collapses from ~0.75% to 0.20–0.30%. The bot is already at it. Every number below is the corrected run.

Executive conclusion
Do not raise risk. The evidence supports lowering it — or halting.

On the only genuinely untouched period, the strategy loses money at every risk level from 0.1% to 3.0%, and raising risk makes the loss and the drawdown strictly worse. On the in-sample periods where it does look profitable, a concurrency-adjusted growth-optimal calculation puts the right fraction at 0.09–0.30% — bracketing, and mostly below, the 0.3% already in production. There is no window, no cost assumption and no metric in this study under which a higher number is defensible.

This is a research output. Nothing was deployed and config.yaml was not modified.

Correction 1 · you were right

Execution cost, measured from real fills

The repo has never measured its own costs. It carries two different hardcoded constants — 18 bps in the main engine, 12 bps baked into a second "validated" dataset — and a third figure of 34.1 bps from an indirect calibration. You said the cost should be normal and obtainable from trade data. With the database you provided, it now is.

The bot's exec_log table holds 176 closed live trades. Stop-outs are the clean estimator: a stop-out should realise exactly −1.0 R before friction, so the shortfall below −1.0 is the entire round trip. Across 133 real stop-outs, trades realised −1.2275 R against a mean stop distance of 1.40% of price.

The whole study runs at the measured 24.2 bps, with 18 and 34.1 as sensitivity bounds. Two caveats I cannot remove: the 133 fills span only 8 days (2026-08-03 → 08-11), and "excess loss beyond −1 R" bundles genuine stop gapping in with fees and spread. Both push the estimate high, so 24.2 is a mild upper bound on pure friction — and a fair estimate of what the account actually pays.

Lowering the cost does not change the answer. The breakeven round trip — the cost at which mean net R crosses zero — is +70.7 bps on DEV and +68.2 bps on VAL, but −35.3 bps on the holdout. A negative breakeven means the holdout loses money at zero cost. The out-of-sample problem is gross alpha, not friction.

Correction 2

Bugs found, and what changed

Four defects were found by independent audit agents. Two were fixed and every affected result was re-run on the corrected engine; two are recorded but not fixed, because fixing them properly needs data that does not exist.

FixedClose batches applied in insertion order, not exit order

backtest_production_correct.py:377 iterated open_positions in the order positions were opened. Whenever several closed in the same batch — 2,106 batches on this universe, 67% of them mis-ordered, largest batch 52 — PnL, peak/drawdown updates and the 20-trade regime window were applied out of chronological order. Final balance is unaffected (addition commutes) but every path-dependent quantity was wrong. An isolated three-trade reproduction reported 50.33% maxDD against a true 34.22%. Fixed by sorting the batch on exit_time, in both the main loop and the end-of-data flush. Mean absolute drawdown shift across all 52 cells: 2.0 points; maximum 23.6.

Fixed upstreamCHOP gate read the entry bar's own candle

Found two days before this study and already corrected in the working tree, but the on-disk trade universe still carried the leak. I rebuilt it from klines with the honest ch[bos] gate — 32,929 trades — and every number here comes from that rebuild, not the stale file.

OpenMark-to-market equity interpolates toward each trade's known final PnL

backtest_production_correct.py:535 marks open positions by linearly interpolating toward pos['pnl'] — the trade's already-resolved outcome. Under the live config (size_basis='equity') that leaks future information into the sizing of other concurrent trades, and it structurally floors intra-trade drawdown. Both the audit agent and the independent replication agent found this separately. Direction matters: it biases drawdown down at high risk, so the case for raising risk looks better than it is. Correcting it requires intrabar marks the trade table does not contain.

OpenRight-censoring near the data boundary

Unresolved trades are silently dropped. Because targets sit 3–10× farther than stops, slow winners vanish near any boundary while fast losers remain — worth +0.29 R/trade in a controlled test at an artificial 2024 cutoff. This makes the holdout look worse than it is, and I correct for it explicitly below rather than leaving it in my favour.

The mechanism

Why 0.3% is already too much

The simulator says what happened. This says what had to happen, from the net-R distribution alone, with no engine involved — so the two can be checked against each other.

 The sequential column is the number that would justify raising risk — and it is the wrong number for this bot. Because dozens of correlated positions settle against the same equity before any of them compounds, the relevant bet is the cluster, not the trade. Ranges span bucket choices from 12 to 48 hours.

The one open disagreement in revision 1 is now resolved. The red team flagged that cluster-Kelly (0.09–0.30%) and the simulator's own ROI/DD optimum (0.75–1.5%) disagreed by 4–8×, and I recorded it as unresolved dissent. With the live exit rule modelled, cluster-Kelly gives 0.10–0.22% and the simulator peaks at 0.20–0.30%. Two independent methods, one analytic and one path-simulated, now agree within a factor of about 1.5. The earlier gap was not a disagreement about sizing — it was both methods correctly describing an exit rule the bot no longer uses.

Monte Carlo agrees on the mechanism: preserving weekly clustering in a block bootstrap inflates the probability of a 50% loss from 8.7% to 47.5% versus i.i.d. resampling — a 5.4× understatement in any analysis that treats these trades as independent.

The constraint the backtests cannot see

Margin and liquidation

The bot sets leverage to the exchange maximum on every symbol, holds no cap on concurrent positions, and — verified by the absence of any switch-isolated call or tradeMode field in autobot/brokers/bybit.py — runs a cross-margin unified account. All open positions draw on one equity pool. The engine models initial margin at entry but has no concept of maintenance margin or liquidation price: a grep for liquidat|maintenance|mmr across all 1,060 lines returns nothing. It cannot represent an account blowup.

Adversarial review

What the red team broke, and what held

Landed — the draft was overstated in three places

  • The concurrency-adjusted Kelly figure is a range (0.09–0.30%), not the point estimate of 0.17% I first computed; a resample-anchor bug in my own script was found and fixed.
  • The simulator's best-ROI/DD f (0.75–1.5%) disagrees with cluster-Kelly by 4–8×. I side with Kelly — the simulator's optimum sits at 92% drawdown and moves under a bug fix — but the disagreement is real and unresolved.
  • Right-censoring makes the holdout look worse than it is. Corrected, holdout gross alpha is −0.09 R, not −0.27; at a 30-day truncation it is +0.00. The honest claim is "the edge is at best zero and does not clear its own costs", not "the edge is dead."

Failed — the central claim survived every attack

  • Is the holdout just noise? Weekly block-bootstrap 95% CI on holdout net R is [−0.86, −0.12], excluding zero. The DEV-minus-holdout gap CI is [+0.27, +1.19], P(gap ≤ 0) < 0.1%.
  • Is it just a bad patch? It ranks worst of 156 overlapping two-month windows in three years on net R, third-worst on gross. The one historical comparable was followed by another negative window, not a recovery.
  • Is the cost estimate wrong? Re-measured independently: 24.2 bps, CI [19.8, 28.7]. Moot regardless — the holdout's breakeven cost is negative.
  • Does the independent engine agree? A from-scratch simulator, sharing no code, reproduced monotonically rising drawdown with f (Spearman 0.98–1.00) and negative holdout ROI at every f. It disagreed on exact in-sample ROI at 0.5–2%, traced to the open mark-to-market bug.

Confidence

Why this could still be wrong

High confidence that risk should not be raised — it is over-determined, surviving every attack from four independent directions. Moderate confidence in the specific 0.10–0.15% figure, which rests on a Kelly range and an in-sample edge I do not believe is real.

  • The strongest objection is epistemic, not statistical. This study replays today's rule set uniformly across three years that were never traded under it — several walk-forward refits, the CHOP fix, the BOS-timing fix, the trailing stop. A direct parity check of the reconstruction against the days exec_log actually covers is the highest-value follow-up and was outside scope.
  • The holdout is 2 months and 2,509 trades. It is statistically significant on a block bootstrap, but it is still one regime.
  • The mark-to-market leak remains open, and it biases drawdown down at high risk. Correcting it would strengthen this conclusion, not weaken it.
  • The backtest's signal detector is not the live bot's: 11.7% of backtest signals are ones live would never generate, due to a dedup-ordering difference. Direction is conservative here, but it means no script in this repo replays the live signal stream exactly.
  • Slippage was not directly measurable — slippage_frac and fee_usd are unpopulated in exec_log, so cost was inferred from stop-out shortfall rather than read from fills.
Method

Agents, and who checked whom

Nine agents were spawned. Every load-bearing finding was reviewed by at least one other agent, and disagreements were resolved by code inspection and rerun rather than by vote.

SPEC
Traced the sizing chain to source; produced the config difference table. Found no concurrent-position limit exists anywhere in the live path.
ENGINE
Audited the simulator. Found the close-batch ordering bug and the mark-to-market leak. Cleared compounding, margin release and cost sequencing.
LEAK
Found and quantified right-censoring (+0.29 R at a controlled cutoff) and the backtest-vs-live signal-detector divergence. Cleared indicators, BOS anchoring and tie resolution.
LIQ
Established cross-margin by absence of evidence; computed the correlated-loss ruin thresholds and the already-live margin exhaustion.
COST
Independently estimated 28 bps central, 18–45 range — bracketing my 24.2 bps measurement. Found the repo's second undocumented 12 bps constant.
MC
Block-bootstrap drawdown distributions and risk of ruin. Flagged loudly that its simulator omits the live caps, so its ruin figures are an upper bound — not taken at face value here.
WF
99 walk-forward runs. Produced the 0.228 rank correlation that independently defeats the tuning premise.
REPL
From-scratch simulator sharing no code. Confirmed drawdown monotonicity and the holdout result; its disagreement on in-sample ROI corroborated the mark-to-market bug.
RED
Adversarial. Broke three sub-claims, forced the Kelly point estimate into a range, and failed to break the central conclusion.

Dissent, and how it resolved

  • Cluster-Kelly vs simulator optimum — Resolved in rev 2 A 4–8× gap in revision 1, closed to ~1.5× once the live trailing exit was modelled. Neither method was wrong; both were correctly describing a fixed-TP rule the bot stopped using on 2026-08-02.
  • Cost — my 24.2 bps measurement against the cost agent's 28 bps central estimate. Overlapping intervals; immaterial to the conclusion.
  • The trailing stop itself — Open, and larger than this study Under the honest CHOP gate the trail costs 0.187 R/trade and cuts win-adjusted expectancy by ~76%. It was deployed as drawdown control, not as a profit upgrade, and the repo's own notes already withdrew its return claim. But a rule that quarters the edge deserves its own review — it is now the largest single lever on this bot, and it was not in this study's scope.
