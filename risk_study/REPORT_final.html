Variant bake-off · 2026-08-12

Which version of this bot actually grows $1,500?

Ten configurations, twelve feature toggles, two independent engines, an adversarial review and a formal debate with an adjudicating judge. Same signals, same bars, same measured costs throughout — only the configuration changes.

$1,500 · 0.3% risk · 24.2 bps measured 2023-06-01 → 2026-07-25 leak-free sizing 14 agents total
Verdict — carried with amendment
Make the change. But buy it knowing you are buying survivability, not growth.

The configuration deployed today is the worst arm tested on almost every axis: $5,744 final, −48.4% over the untouched holdout, and its entire profit rests on the top 1% of trades five times over. Switching to one global rr=10 / atr_mult=3.0 with the trail kept on and the 21-day halt adopted gives $17,205, the lowest drawdown of any arm (47.0%), and the only positive holdout in the study (+15.8%).

It does not give the most dollars. Reverting to the pre-2026-08-02 bot — fitted parameters with the old fixed take-profit — makes $91,223. Why that is not the recommendation is the single most important thing on this page.

The mechanism

Parameters and exit rule are not independent choices

This is the finding that reorganised the whole study. The exit rule does not have a fixed cost or benefit — it depends entirely on the parameters it is paired with.

Fixed take-profits3_a1 trailing stopEffect of the trail
Fitted per-symbol (rr 3–10, atr 1.0–2.0)$91,223$5,744÷ 15.9
Global rr=10 / atr=3.0$810$17,493× 21.6
 The trail destroys the fitted configuration and rescues the global one. Two mechanisms, both verified: the trail arms at +3R, so any rr = 3 config's take-profit sits exactly at the arming threshold and the trail can never fire — measured at 0.0% arming, confirmed exactly against the bot's own live shadow data. And at atr_mult = 3 the take-profit sits 30 ATR away and is essentially unreachable (win rate 10.3%), so without a trail there is nothing to bank. config.yaml's own note already said it: "a bare trail needs to be LATE and WIDE."

The trail resolver behind all of this passed a four-way parity check — the live engine Bot4H._trail_one, the shadow learner, the repo's validated reference, and this study's implementation agree on 100.00% of 16,049 trades, on both the R outcome and the exit bar.

Adversarial process

The debate, and what the judge ruled

A proponent and an opponent each argued the motion with access to all the data, then an independent judge adjudicated by going back to the numbers rather than weighing the rhetoric.

Rulings for the change

  • Comparator. The motion keeps the trail, so the baseline is what runs today (fitted+trail), not the retired fixed-TP bot.
  • Outlier robustness. Trimming the best 5% of trades: global degrades to −0.061 R, old base to −0.260 R. The genuinely fragile arm is the current one, which goes negative after 1% removed.
  • Contamination charge rejected for the headline statistic: the transfer test ran on all four divergence types across all live symbols — 420,953 rows including the 376 rejected pairs — not just the selected 728.
  • Engine disagreement changes magnitudes, not ranking. Both engines agree current is worst, global has the lowest drawdown, and global is the only arm positive on holdout.

Rulings against, and concessions

  • Old base deserves a seat. Reverting the trail is a real alternative that makes 5× the money and wins 8 of 8 restart dates — it was not dismissed, it was outranked on risk.
  • atr_mult = 3.0 is outside the grid the bot's own optimiser ever searched. Restricted to ≤2.0, every global arm's holdout is negative.
  • The halt is not a clean win. The backtest models it as an instant hard filter; live it is advisory and needs the operator to act. A prior rolling test found it helped in only 2 of 10 windows.
  • Three of four walk-forward fold confidence intervals include zero.
The falsifier

The judge named one test that would flip the verdict. I ran it.

If the global parameters carry a real edge, they should work on the 376 (symbol, divergence type) pairs the walk-forward rejected — where no selection ever happened.

The falsifier partially fires, and this is the honest limit of the finding. On pairs where no selection ever occurred, the global configuration's net R is negative — −0.042 over the full history, −0.180 in the holdout — though both confidence intervals include zero. The global parameters therefore do not create alpha on the unselected population. They exploit the existing pair set better than the fitted parameters do.

That is still a real reason to make the change — you are keeping those pairs either way, and this is a strictly better way to trade them. But it means the change inherits, rather than escapes, the dependency on a pair selection whose own validity remains unproven.

The honest frame
This is damage control, not a growth plan.

Every headline number here still sits on a strategy whose unselected parameter grid is net-negative at every reward-to-risk setting, and whose standing verdict — from an adversarial review nine days ago — is that its honest alpha is below its execution cost. Nothing in this bake-off overturns that. What it establishes is narrower and still worth acting on: the configuration currently deployed is measurably the worst way to run this strategy, and there is a strictly better one available for the price of editing a config file.
