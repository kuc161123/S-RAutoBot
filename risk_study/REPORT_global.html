Symbol & parameter study · phase 1 · 2026-08-11

One global RR/ATR beats 728 fitted per-symbol configs

Month-by-month from $1,500, running the bot's exact live machinery. The only thing that changes between arms is which (rr, atr_mult) each trade uses.

same signals · same trades · same exit rule 0.3% risk · 24.2 bps · s3_a1 trail 2023-06 → 2026-07
What the data shows
$8,587 → $20,775, with drawdown falling from 65% to 47%.

Replacing every per-symbol fitted (rr, atr_mult) with a single global rr = 10, atr_mult = 3.0 — same symbols, same divergence types, same signals — produces 2.4× the ending equity on 17 points less drawdown. It is also the only arm that is positive across the untouched holdout: +8.3% where the live config lost 43.4%.

This clears the first of six pre-registered criteria. It is a strong signal, not yet a recommendation — see the checklist at the bottom for what is still untested.

Addendum — the parameter result is real, but it is second-order
The reason the fitted config loses is the trailing stop, not the parameters.

Running each parameter set under both exit rules separates the two effects, and it inverts the story. Under a fixed take-profit the live fitted config is the best arm tested (+0.246 R vs +0.170 for the best global). Under the trail it is one of the worst (+0.058). The trail costs the live config −0.187 R/trade — three to six times what it costs any global arm — and it is the only reason a global choice wins at all.

Verification — closed

Four independent implementations of the trail agree on every trade

The earlier draft of this page flagged an unresolved gap: this study's trail resolver appeared to diverge from the bot's live shadow at rr=10, exactly where the claim lived. That has now been run down properly, against the repo's own parity harness rather than against a two-week live sample.

So the apparent divergence was in the live sample, not the code. The trailing-stop finding therefore stands. Under the honest CHOP gate the trail costs the live config −0.187 R/trade. Note what this does not license: turning the trail off is a separate change with its own drawdown consequences, and the arm that wins every test on this page (global rr=10/atr=3) keeps the trail on — the trail helps that configuration by +0.062 R. The problem was never the trail alone; it was the trail paired with fitted parameters it was never matched to.

Not cherry-picked

Six of eight global choices beat the fitted config

I chose rr=10/atr=3 after seeing the ranking, so the obvious objection is selection. The defence is that the gradient is smooth and monotone in both parameters, and most of the grid wins — this is a plateau, not a spike.

 The live config ranks sixth of nine on ending equity and has the joint-worst drawdown. The ordering is monotone in both parameters — higher RR is better, wider stops are better — with no local spikes, which is the signature of a real effect rather than a fit.

There is a mechanism, and the config file already predicted it. Execution cost in R is round_trip / (atr_mult × ATR/price), so a wider stop mechanically dilutes cost. More importantly, config.yaml's own trailing-stop note says "a bare trail needs to be LATE and WIDE." With atr_mult = 3 the initial stop is wide and the +3R arming trigger is far away, so the trail arms late and stops shaking out of winners — exactly the design intent. Note that atr_mult 3.0 sits outside the live config's range, which caps at 2.0. The bot could not have found this by refitting within its own grid.

Expanding the universe is a different change, and it loses. Trading all four divergence types on all 277 symbols with the same global parameters gives $2,778 and a 74% drawdown. So the live config's choice of which (symbol, type) pairs to trade does carry value in-sample; it is only its choice of rr and atr_mult per pair that is harmful. These two effects are separated deliberately here so they are not confused for each other.

Honest status

What this does and does not establish

PROTOCOL_symbols.md was written before any of this ran and fixed six criteria for recommending a change. All six are now met.

✓
Beats A0 on the untouched holdout. +6.9% vs −44.8% (independent replication: +1.9% vs −53.7%).
✓
Beats A0 in ≥3 of 4 walk-forward folds — wins 4 of 4, plus the holdout. Fold diffs +0.258, +0.061, +0.165, +0.032 R.
✓
Beats the random null. Against 2,000 random per-pair (rr, atr) assignments, A1 sits at the 100th percentile (p = 0.0005). Correcting for having picked the best of 8: best-of-8 beats A0 in 100% of weekly-block resamples, margin CI [+0.056, +0.201] — excludes zero.
✓
Beats every market control on ROI/DD, full period and holdout: BTC buy-and-hold, a 277-symbol long-only basket, and a BTC 200-EMA trend follower. A0 passes full-period but fails all three on the holdout.
✓
Not concentrated. Trimming the top 0.5/1/2/5% of trades from both arms leaves the A1−A0 gap at +0.125 to +0.147 R — it widens rather than narrows. A1's top 1% of trades is 8.5% of its positive R, against A0's 8.8%.
✓
Independently reproduced. A second agent rebuilt signals, exits and the portfolio simulator from raw klines, sharing no code. Same sign on all three headline claims; A1 drawdown within 1.1 points.

Two disagreements, resolved by inspection rather than vote. The null-test agent reproduced every number here exactly but recorded criteria 3 and 5 as FAIL — it applied them to A0 rather than to the challenger. PROTOCOL_symbols.md line 97 reads "Recommend changing the bot only if a challenger arm satisfies all of", so the criteria attach to A1; the verdicts above follow the protocol as written. Its criterion-5 "hostile" variant (strip A1's best trades while leaving A0's intact) fails at 2% removal, but that asymmetric test is a stress I invented, not the pre-registered one.

The single most damaging number in this study. A0 — the bot's fitted per-symbol configuration, the output of its walk-forward optimisation — sits at the 0.0th percentile of 2,000 random parameter assignments drawn from the same eight-combo grid. Not merely no better than chance: worse than essentially every coin flip. Two independent implementations agree on this to four decimal places.

The dollar figures are optimistic and are not what the account did. Every arm inherits the (symbol, div_type) pairs the walk-forward selected in-sample, so all of them carry that selection premium — the comparison between arms is fair, the absolute levels are not. And the live account is near $920 today, not $8,587: this is a reconstruction of what the current rule set would have done over three years it was never actually traded through.

  • The holdout is six weeks. It can refute a strong claim; it cannot establish one. A1's +6.9% (+1.9% replicated) rests on a small sample and could be luck — the walk-forward folds and the random null are what carry the case, not the holdout alone.
  • Nothing here makes the strategy's underlying edge real. The raw unselected grid is net-negative at every RR (−0.102 at rr=2 to −0.038 at rr=10). This finding is about cutting a self-inflicted loss, not about finding alpha.
  • Three of the four walk-forward fold differences have confidence intervals that include zero; only F1 and the holdout exclude it. The 4-of-4 record is directionally consistent but individually thin.
  • The independent replication got A0 at $2,984 against this study's $8,587 — a 2.9× gap on the incumbent arm, attributed to path-dependent interaction between anti-pyramid blocking, the regime multiplier and compounding. The direction replicates; absolute dollars do not.
  • The out-of-universe question cannot be answered honestly at all: of 231 non-live symbols in the cache, 188 are delisted and only 43 still trade. Any "should we add symbols" test is 81% survivorship-biased.
  • Deploying this changes the bot's trade profile: 32,102 trades instead of 32,913, longer holds, a much wider stop on every position. Position notional per dollar of risk falls (wider stop = smaller size), which eases the margin-exhaustion problem — but that interaction was not separately tested.
